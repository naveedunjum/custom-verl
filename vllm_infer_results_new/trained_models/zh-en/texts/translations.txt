Information Expansion Technology "Chinese Encoding Character Set"
This document specifies the Chinese graphic characters used in information technology and their hexadecimal representation in binary code.
This document applies to the processing, exchange, storage, transmission, display, input, and output of information containing Chinese characters and other graphical and symbolic characters.
This document applies to technical products with functions for the information processing and exchange of Chinese and other text graphics characters. It includes, but is not limited to, software products represented by input methods, optical character recognition (OCR), editing and proofreading, machine translation, speech synthesis, text transcription, and intelligent writing, as well as portable products represented by computers, communication terminals, e-readers, and learning devices.

4 Vocabulary
4.1 Summary
The characters included in this document are encoded using single-byte, double-byte, or four-byte encoding.
The single-byte section includes all 128 characters from 0x00 to 0x7F as specified in GB/T 11383-1989.
4.3 Two-Byte Section
The double-byte section includes all graphic characters from GB/T 2312-1980, CJK Unified Ideographs from GB/T 13000, as well as some additional graphic characters.
The characters in the double-byte section shall be in accordance with the provisions of Appendix A.
Among these, the graphic representation, code position, and function of the ideographic descriptor should comply with the specifications in Appendix B.
Note: GB/T 13000 unifies the encoding of Chinese characters used in countries and regions such as China, Japan, Korea, and Vietnam.
Chinese characters with unique abstract shapes are assigned separate code positions; Chinese characters with the same abstract shape but different origins are assigned shared code positions.
The encoded Chinese characters are called CJK Unified Ideographs, where CJK stands for China, Japan, and Korea.
4.4 Four-Byte Section
The four-byte section includes 66 CJK Unified Ideographs from GB/T 13000 outside of the above two-byte characters (from 9FA6 to 9FEF, excluding the 8 characters from 9FB4 to 9FBB), as well as CJK Unified Ideographs Extension A, CJK Unified Ideographs Extension B, CJK Unified Ideographs Extension C, CJK Unified Ideographs Extension D, CJK Unified Ideographs Extension E, CJK Unified Ideographs Extension F, and characters of minority languages that have already been encoded in GB/T 13000.
The characters in the four-byte section are governed by the provisions of Appendix C.
5. Overall Structure
In the main text, numbers prefixed with 0x represent hexadecimal notation, while those without the 0x prefix are in decimal notation.
All encoded representations in the appendix are in hexadecimal, and other numerical representations are in decimal.
The single-byte part uses the encoding structure specified in GB/T 11383-1989, using code positions from 0x00 to 0x7F.
The double-byte GB code uses two eight-bit binary byte sequences to represent a character. The first byte ranges from 0x81 to 0xFE, and the second byte ranges from 0x40 to 0x7E and 0x80 to 0xFE.
The four-byte section uses the 0x30-0x39 range (as defined in GB/T 11383-1989) as a suffix for the extension of double-byte encoding. The encoding range is from 0x81308130 to 0xFE39FE39.
The first byte of a four-byte character has an encoding range of 0x81 to 0xFE; the second byte has an encoding range of 0x30 to 0x39; the third byte also has an encoding range of 0x81 to 0xFE; and the fourth byte has an encoding range of 0x30 to 0x39. That is: 0x81308130 to 0x81308139; 0x81308230 to 0x81308239; 0x8130FEFE30 to 0x8130FE39; 0x81318130 to 0x81318139; 0xFE39FE30 to 0xFE39FE39.

9.1 General Rules
This document specifies three levels of implementation.
System software products that comply with the corresponding implementation level should provide input and output functions for all characters within the scope of that implementation level.
9.2 Implementation Level 1
Implement level 1 support for the single-byte encoding section, the double-byte encoding section, and the four-byte encoding section of the CJK Unified Ideographs (i.e., 0x82358F33 to 0x82359636) and the CJK Unified Ideographs Extension A (i.e., 0x8139EE39 to 0x82358738).
Any product subject to this document should meet the requirements for Level 1.
Note: According to the software application requirements, level 1 may also choose any one or a combination of non-Chinese scripts listed in Table 3.
9.3 Implementation Level 2
Level 2 implementation includes Level 1 implementation.
Furthermore, level 2 includes all the encoded Chinese characters in the "General Chinese Character Set" that are not included in level 1.
The code positions and character forms of the Chinese characters included in the "General Standardized Chinese Characters Table" in this document are shown in Appendix A.
System software and supporting software should meet the requirements of implementation level 2.
Note: System software and support software include but are not limited to operating systems, database management systems, middleware (information on software product classification is referenced from GB/T 36475).
9.4 Implementation Level 3
The implementation of level 3 includes the implementation of level 2.
Furthermore, achieving level 3 includes all Chinese characters specified in this document and the Kangxi radicals listed in Table 3.
The products used for government services and public services should meet the requirements of level 3.
Note: The service industry, including government services, covers but is not limited to the railway transportation industry, road transportation industry, water transportation industry, air transportation industry, multimodal transport and transport agency industry, postal industry, financial services, insurance industry, land and management industry, healthcare, state organs, social security, etc. (Industry classification information is referenced to GB/T 4754)."

Executive Summary
The Impact of COVID-19 on the Drug Market
Its impact may be similar to the 2008 economic crisis.
The global COVID-19 pandemic has plunged the world into an unprecedented crisis.
In many countries, the disease has overwhelmed healthcare systems, pushing them to the brink of collapse. Restrictive measures implemented by governments have caused severe disruptions in the global economy.
The impact of this pandemic on the drug market is still unclear and difficult to predict, but it may have far-reaching effects.
Some producers may be forced to seek new methods to manufacture drugs because restricting movement also limits access to precursors and basic chemicals.
Smugglers may have to find new routes and methods because they are unable to cross the border due to travel restrictions.
The patterns of drug use and distribution may change, and the government's capacity to respond may also be hindered.
The changes brought by the novel coronavirus pandemic are unprecedented, but we can still draw some lessons and insights from past crises.
After the 2008 financial crisis, some drug users began seeking cheaper synthetic substances, shifting their method of use toward injecting drugs.
At the same time, governments around the world have cut budgets related to drugs.
If governments in all countries respond to the current economic recession in the same way, preventive measures and interventions against drug use and related risky behaviors, as well as drug treatment services, may face severe setbacks.
Furthermore, the priority of intercepting operations and international cooperation may decrease, making traffickers more capable of acting.
Some countries face large-scale drug smuggling through commercial flights, and it is expected that these countries will be most directly affected by drug trafficking activities.
Messengers have been found to carry synthetic drugs such as methamphetamine in their personal backpacks or hidden in their private luggage, sometimes in large quantities. Due to restrictions in almost all regions on air travel, the activity of supplying drugs through commercial air travel may be completely disrupted.
Although the international postal supply chain has also been disrupted, the activities of trafficking through the dark web and cargo mail may increase.
In the long term, economic downturns and related containment measures may disrupt the drug market.
Due to the rising unemployment rate and lack of opportunities, the poor and vulnerable groups are more likely to use drugs in harmful ways, develop drug addiction, and turn to illegal activities related to drugs—whether production or transportation.
Drug trafficking organizations may take advantage of this situation to provide services to vulnerable groups and recruit new members to expand their ranks.
As the government's capacity to respond decreases, these changes may quickly take root and become a new reality for many communities.
Expansion and Complexity
Market Growth
The population growth pattern partially explains the reasons for market expansion.
The phenomenon of drug use around the world has been increasing, both in terms of total numbers and in terms of the proportion of drug users in the world's population.
In 2009, it was estimated that there were 210 million drug users, accounting for 4.8% of the global population aged 15-64. By 2018, the number had increased to 269 million, representing 5.3% of that population.
In the past two decades, the rate of increase in drug use in developing countries has been significantly faster than in developed countries.
This to some extent reflects the differences in overall population growth during this period—7% in developed countries and 28% in developing countries—and also reflects the rapid growth of young populations in developing countries.
Teenagers and young adults account for the largest proportion among drug users.
During the period from 2000 to 2018, the population of this age group in developing countries increased by 16%, while that in developed countries decreased by 10%.
Urbanization is a driving force for the current and future drug markets.
Whether it is developed countries or developing countries, the rate of drug use in urban areas is higher than in rural areas.
The increase in drug use is partly due to the large-scale movement of people from rural areas to urban areas. Currently, more than half of the world's population lives in urban areas, compared to 34% in 1960.
Urbanization may be a key factor in the future dynamics of the drug market, especially in developing countries where the trend of urbanization is more pronounced.
The increase in wealth is associated with a rise in drug use, but the people who bear the greatest burden of disease are the poorest around the world. Drug use is more prevalent in developed countries than in developing countries.
Drugs like cocaine are even more closely linked to wealthier regions around the world.
Similarly, within each country, the prevalence of drug use is higher among the socio-economic elite.
However, individuals with lower socioeconomic status have a higher rate of developing drug addiction disorders.
Data from a few countries suggest a link between harmful drug use patterns and low income and illness.
These patterns seem less common in more affluent social classes.
The economic constraints caused by the global COVID-19 crisis are likely to exacerbate the risks faced by the most vulnerable groups, including drug users.
For example, changes in the labor market, such as rising unemployment rates, have been associated with an increase in drug use in the past. However, this pandemic has already forced hundreds of millions of people around the world to lose their jobs.
The outbreak of COVID-19 may lead to further expansion of the drug market. The pandemic could result in more farmers increasing or engaging in the cultivation of illicit crops, either due to reduced control capabilities of national authorities or because, under economic crisis conditions, more people may be forced to take risks and engage in illegal activities.

The terms of the "price guarantee agreement" are definitely important.

The images sent to the journalists show that the developer of the project has promised that if a buyer purchases a unit between February 1st and February 28th, the developer can offer the buyer a house exchange and refund the price difference from the date of signing the purchase and sale contract until the delivery of the corresponding unit. If the discount of other units in the project is greater than the discount enjoyed by the buyer when purchasing the unit (limited to other unsold units of the same floor plan, same building, same unit, and same property type), the developer can exchange the house and refund the price difference.
Mr. Sun said that the sales consultant explained that this is essentially a "price protection agreement," aimed at reassuring buyers that they can sell the property without worrying about the developer lowering prices for promotion. There are very few properties that buyers can compare prices for, so the "barriers" are still quite high.
Some homebuyers believe that due to information asymmetry, sellers may offer additional discounts during sales, making it difficult for buyers to protect their rights.

After April 2011, nine residential projects in Nanjing also offered some units with the promise of "price difference compensation."

1964 Alaska Earthquake
March 27, 1964 was a Friday, which was also Good Friday.
On this day, a earthquake occurred in Alaska, USA, which is the largest earthquake in North America's history, with a moment magnitude of 9.2.
Until 2006, the Alaska earthquake remained the third largest earthquake in recorded history.
When the earthquake occurred, a fault between the Pacific Plate and the North American Plate ruptured near College Fjord in Prince William Sound.
In most areas, the earthquake lasted between 3 to 5 minutes.
The tsunami caused by the sea bed displacement reached a height of 67 meters.
Because it was a holiday that day, schools and many companies were closed.
This massive earthquake ultimately resulted in 139 deaths.
